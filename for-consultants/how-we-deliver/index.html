How We Deliver a Facade

From the first drawing to the warranty certificate: who does the engineering, what we submit, how the steel substructure and the stone are installed, and what you receive at handover.

General guidance. Each project is designed by its engineer or consultant. Updated: November 2026

In 30 seconds
  • Two engineering routes: the shop drawings are issued to us, or the project's facade engineer prepares them within our scope.
  • The order of work: submittals and samples, mock-up on request, steel substructure, stone, inspection, handover.
  • At handover you receive the records and the 5-year written workmanship warranty attested by the Chamber of Commerce, issued by the contracting company.

Scope

What We Do

We supply and install mechanically fixed natural stone (mainly Riyadh limestone), marble, granite and porcelain facades. We also install the steel substructure behind them, to the engineer's design. Installation is by our own team with a site supervisor.

The fixing systems we install are Up-Down angles, pin (PEN) fixing, C-Channel, kerf rail and undercut anchors. They are compared on the fixing systems page.

Engineering

Two Engineering Routes

Route AWe install to your approved shop drawings

The consultant or the main contractor supplies approved shop drawings. We install to them and raise any site conflict before proceeding.

Route BEngineering within our scope

The project's facade engineer prepares the design basis, wind loads per the current SBC 301, calculations for the steel substructure, shop drawings and a part list, and visits the site weekly.

On both routes the design belongs to the project engineer or consultant, and the installation follows the approved documents.

Under the Law of Practising Engineering Professions (Art. 8), accredited engineers write their name, professional grade and accreditation number on their engineering works [1]. Wind loads are calculated for each project to the current SBC 301 [2]; the calculation states the code edition used.

Step by step

From Enquiry to Warranty

  1. Enquiry and drawings

    We review the elevations, the stone, the backup wall and the heights, and say which fixing systems suit them. You can start with the send drawings form.

  2. Engineering route agreed

    Route A (your approved shop drawings) or Route B (shop drawings and calculations by the project's facade engineer, within our scope) is fixed before pricing is final.

  3. Material submittals and samples

    Stone samples that show the range, and data for the fixings, brackets and anchors, are submitted for approval. Bracket material is stainless steel or hot-dip galvanised steel, according to the project specification.

  4. Mock-up On request

    Built from the approved materials and drawings, by the same crew, and kept as the benchmark once approved.

  5. Setting out

    Grid and level lines are set and the wall is checked against the drawings before any anchor is drilled.

  6. Steel substructure

    Wall brackets, channels or frames are installed to the engineer's design. Anchor and bolt sizes are per the engineer's calculation and the consultant's specification.

  7. Anchor pull-out tests On request

    Arranged with the anchor manufacturer or a testing laboratory, and witnessed by the consultant.

  8. Stone installation

    By our own team with a site supervisor, panel by panel to the approved drawings: fixing engagement, clearances and joints are checked as the work rises.

  9. Engineer's site visits Route B

    On Route B projects the facade engineer visits the site weekly.

  10. Handover

    Final inspection and snagging with the consultant, the project records, and the maintenance and handover guide.

  11. Warranty

    A 5-year written workmanship warranty attested by the Chamber of Commerce, issued by the contracting company.

Behind the stone

The Steel Substructure

We install the steel substructure; the engineer designs it. These are the points a design basis for a substructure normally settles, with the published guidance behind them. They are general guidance, not project values.

  • Wind. Wind loads on the stone and its supports are calculated for each project to the current SBC 301, and the design pressures are stated on the documents [2].
  • Stiffness. Stone tolerates very little movement of its support. ASTM C1242 treats a backup more flexible than L/600 as a case for a stone specialist [3]; the limit used is the engineer's decision.
  • Anchors to concrete. The steel of the bolt is only one check. A steel connection program does not prove the concrete side: pull-out, bond and splitting come from the anchor's own technical data [4]. A separate anchor calculation for the actual product is part of the design.
  • Hollow block. Anchors in hollow masonry have low capacity in the maker's published data [5], and one maker states it has no test data for the top of an ungrouted hollow wall [6]. Loadbearing brackets and frames go to reinforced concrete unless the engineer proves otherwise by tests.
  • Corrosion protection. Hot-dip galvanising is specified to a standard with a minimum coating thickness [7]; hollow sections need vent and drain holes [8]; site cuts and welds remove the zinc and are repaired by a named method [9].
  • Dissimilar metals. Stainless fasteners on larger galvanised or carbon steel parts cause little problem; the reverse arrangement is the dangerous one. Where the joint can stay damp, the metals are separated with isolating washers or pads [10][11].
  • Movement. Steel moves roughly two to two and a half times as much as stone with temperature [12]. Each member has a fixed point and sliding points, and the stone has movement joints where the engineer places them.
  • Tolerances. Structural steel tolerances are looser than stone tolerances [13], so the adjustment range is designed into the bracket between frame and stone.

Your side

What Helps Us Help You

  • Architectural elevations and wall sections (PDF or DWG), with building height and storey heights.
  • The stone or panel: type, finish, panel sizes and the thickness you have in mind.
  • The backup wall: reinforced concrete, solid block, hollow block or a mix.
  • The project specification section for stone cladding, if one exists. If not, our guide specification is a starting point.
  • Which engineering route you prefer, A or B.
