Which Thickness, Which Fixing

Panel thickness is not a habit; it follows from how the panel is held and how strong the stone is. This page explains the reasoning with published sources, and gives no project values.

Key points

1.0Short answer

What We Typically Install

  • Natural stone, mainly Riyadh limestone: typically 30 mm, thicker on demand.
  • Marble and granite: often 20 mm.
  • Exterior 20 mm panels on edge fixings (Up-Down, pin, kerf) need the engineer's check and stone tests, or an undercut system.
  • Porcelain: the thickness and the fixing come from the panel maker's system.

Wind loads are calculated for each project to the current SBC 301 [1], and the stone values come from tests on the actual stone. Nothing on this page replaces that calculation.

2.0Edge fixings

Why 30 mm for Pin, Kerf and Up-Down

An edge fixing sits in a hole or slot cut into the panel edge. What holds the panel is the stone left on each side of that hole. Published rules set a minimum for it:

  • The German rule read here (an older edition of DIN 18516-3) asks for at least 10 mm of stone on each side of a pin hole or groove, and 30 mm minimum thickness for pin-fixed panels [2].
  • BS 8298-2, as reported by a stone engineer, asks for 12 mm on each side of a fixing mortice in strong stone and 25 mm in weaker stone, unless testing proves otherwise [3].
  • US industry guidance puts mechanically attached exterior panels at about 32 mm (1¼ in) as a general minimum [4].

Add the hole to 10 mm of stone on each side and the result is 28 to 30 mm. That is why 30 mm is the practical floor for edge fixings, and why a 20 mm panel cannot meet these geometry rules.

3.0Bending

The Square Law

Wind bends the panel between its fixings. For a strip of panel spanning a distance L between two lines of support under a pressure w, textbook beam theory gives the bending stress as:

stress = 0.75 × w × L² ÷ t²

The thickness t is squared. With the same panel and the same wind:

Change in thicknessBending stress becomes
30 mm to 25 mm1.44 times higher
30 mm to 20 mm2.25 times higher

This is a simplification for explanation. Real panels on four point fixings are analysed as plates, and the stone around each fixing is checked separately [5][6]. If either check fails, the answer is thicker stone, a smaller panel, more fixings or a different fixing type.

4.0Safety factors

Published Ranges, Not One Number

Stone varies from block to block, so the factors applied to its tested strength are large. Published values run from 3 to 8 and above, depending on the stone and the document:

The factor used on a project is the engineer's decision under the code named in the specification. Wet strength can be lower than dry strength, and some finishes remove thickness [5][8].

5.020 mm panels

What 20 mm Marble or Granite Needs

Marble and granite are often installed at 20 mm. For exterior panels that is acceptable only when one of these is in place:

  1. Edge fixing with proof. The fixing is outside the published geometry rules above, so it is justified by tests of the fixing in the actual stone (ASTM C1354 or EN 13364, wet and dry) and by the engineer's calculation [10][3]. Expect smaller panels and more fixings.
  2. An undercut system. Undercut anchors with a European Technical Assessment are the documented route for hard stone at 20 mm, with stone-specific resistance from the assessment or from tests, four anchors per panel and gauge-checked holes [9][11].
  3. A thicker panel.

Three more points from the published guidance:

  • Epoxy or resin must not carry the load. A flexible connection is needed; rigid epoxy in kerfs has spalled stone through thermal expansion [12][13].
  • Mesh on the back of stone is fragment retention and handling protection. We found no published source that lets it count toward panel strength.
  • Marble outdoors can bow and lose strength under repeated heating; in documented cases tests projected a large loss of bending strength [14]. A European test exists for it (EN 16306) [15]. For exterior marble in Riyadh sun, ask the engineer whether it is required.

Limestone in undercut systems is a separate case: one maker's data sets 25 to 30 mm minimum for limestone and sandstone, and stone not listed in the assessment needs additional tests [9]. Our typical 30 mm limestone fits that range.

6.0Porcelain

Porcelain Follows the Panel Maker

  • The fixing type depends on the slab thickness. In one slab maker's guide, thin slabs of about 3 to 6 mm use adhesive, clips, rivets or frames, while undercut and kerf fixing need the 12 or 20 mm slabs [16].
  • An undercut anchor assessed for stoneware starts at 8 mm panel thickness, with at least four anchors per panel [17].
  • ISO/TS 17870-3 covers mechanical fixing of large-format porcelain: fixing systems, wind load factors, anchor pull-out and edge fracture tests, impact and anti-shatter mesh [18].
  • Wind and impact performance belongs to the tested combination of panel, fixing and substructure, not to the panel alone [16].
  • Stone habits do not transfer: edge distances and hole depths come from the anchor's assessment.

Our undercut experience on porcelain is with 20 mm panels on Hilti undercut anchors.

  • Reports should be current. US practice as reported asks for data less than three years old; a UK guide accepts older data if the quarry bed has not changed. The project specification decides [20][22].
  • Flexural strength is tested wet and dry, and in both directions of the bedding where it matters [20].
  • "Riyadh limestone" is a trade name covering several quarries and beds. The class and the design strength come from current tests on the actual source, which is why we publish no strength value.
