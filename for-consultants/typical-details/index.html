Typical Fixing Details

Reference drawings of the fixing systems we install: where the anchor sits in the structure, how the bracket carries the panel, and how the load reaches the slabs, beams and columns. They are typical details for discussion at design stage, not for construction.

General guidance. Each project is designed by its engineer or consultant. Updated: November 2026

Load path

Where the Load Goes

Detail 01 · Section

Load path on vertical channels

Principle
The dead load and the wind load of every panel must reach the reinforced concrete structure: slab edges, beams or columns.
Infill walls
Hollow block and other infill walls are not counted on to carry the cladding. Where the wall behind the stone is infill, the brackets go to the concrete and vertical channels span between them.
Fixed and sliding points
Each vertical member has one fixed point that carries its weight, and sliding points that hold it against wind and let it move with temperature. The engineer places them.
Who decides
The load path, the member sizes and spacing and the anchors are the project engineer's design. See how we deliver.
Sections

Five Fixing Systems in Section

One vertical section per system, drawn the same way so they can be compared: wall on the left, stone on the right, numbered parts and a legend. The systems are compared in words on the fixing systems page.

Detail 02 · Section

Up-Down angle fixing

How it carries
The upper panel bears on the blade of the angle and its bottom kerf sits on the up-tongue. The down-tongue restrains the top of the panel below; a clear gap under the blade keeps weight off that panel.
Typical use
Low and medium rise, regular coursing, solid backup wall, stone of 30 mm and more.
Check before approval
Kerf width and depth and the stone left on each side of it; the slotted hole for level adjustment; bracket material per the specification; anchor type per the engineer's calculation.
Detail 03 · Section

PEN (pin) fixing

How it carries
A bracket carries a pin that enters holes drilled in the edges of two adjacent panels. Bottom pins carry weight; top pins restrain against wind.
Typical use
Hand-set stone of 30 mm and more on concrete or solid masonry.
Check before approval
Stone on each side of the hole; a sliding sleeve on one side of the pin; the break-out strength at the hole proven by test in the project stone.
Detail 04 · Section

Vertical C-Channel

How it carries
Wall brackets anchored into slab edges or beams carry vertical steel channels. The stone brackets bolt to the channels, so the infill wall carries nothing.
Typical use
Hollow block or weak infill walls, large cavities and tall storeys.
Check before approval
Calculation of channels, brackets and anchors; one fixed point and sliding points per channel; coating repair at site cuts in galvanised channels; separation of dissimilar metals.
Detail 05 · Section and elevation

Kerf rail system

How it carries
The blade of a horizontal rail sits in a kerf sawn along the top and bottom edges of the panels. The rail is continuous along the row and is fixed to the wall or to vertical profiles.
Typical use
Regular grids of equal panels and rail-based ventilated facades; stone of 30 mm and more.
Check before approval
Kerfs cut in the workshop, not by hand grinder; no rigid epoxy fill in the kerf; rail fixing spacing per the engineer; rail joints and movement.
Detail 06 · Section and elevation

Undercut anchors on rails

How it carries
An anchor locks by shape into an undercut hole in the back of the panel. A hanger on the anchor hooks over a horizontal rail, so each panel hangs on its own and the face stays whole.
Typical use
Thin hard stone, porcelain, large panels, and facades where single panels must be replaceable.
Check before approval
The anchor's assessment covers the stone, or tests are done; holes drilled with the maker's machine and checked with its gauge; number and position of anchors per the assessment and the engineer.
Our experience
Hilti undercut anchors on 30 mm natural stone and on 20 mm porcelain.
Behind the stone

The Steel Substructure in Elevation

Detail 07 · Elevation

Steel substructure

What it is
Vertical steel members fixed to the columns, beams or slab edges. Where the fixing system needs them, horizontal rails are added. We install it; the engineer designs it.
Why it is used
It takes the cladding load past infill walls, spans tall storeys and gives a true plane to set the stone from.
Check before approval
Design basis and wind loads per the current SBC 301; deflection limit of the members; corrosion protection; fixed and sliding points; the adjustment range between structure and stone.
Use of these drawings

Reference, Not Construction

These are typical details, not to scale. On a project, the approved shop drawings by the project engineer or consultant govern. Anchor and bolt sizes are per the engineer's calculation and the consultant's specification; we do not publish sizes. Bracket material is stainless steel or hot-dip galvanised steel, according to the project specification.

To have a detail discussed against your elevations, send us your drawings. The guide specification names the same systems as Options A, B and C.
